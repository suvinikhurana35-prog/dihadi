How this is worked out

The cover works like SEWA's heat insurance for women workers in Ahmedabad: a fixed payout per heatwave once the temperature stays above the trigger for two days in a row, a larger one if it gets more severe, and a limit on payouts a year. Heavy rain uses the India Meteorological Department's categories, 64.5 mm and 115.6 mm in a day.

For the chosen city, Dihadi reads every day from 1980 to 2024 and counts the events that would have paid. It then fits a Poisson regression of events per year against that year's global temperature. Forty-five years is a short record, so the fitted effect of warming is credibility-weighted towards a prior from many Indian cities (or, before that is built, from the IPCC), in proportion to how precise the city's own estimate is.

The yearly payout is a compound distribution (a Poisson number of events, each one severe or not, capped) and is computed exactly, not simulated. The premium is the expected payout, plus a 6% charge on the money held back to survive a 1-in-200 year (the Solvency II standard), plus running costs. The range shown reflects the uncertainty in how strongly warming affects this city. Everyone in one city has the same bad year, so that reserve can't be pooled away inside the group: it has to be held, sponsored or reinsured.

  1. Daily weather: ERA5 reanalysis, ECMWF, via the Open-Meteo historical API (about a 25 km grid).
  2. Global temperature: HadCRUT5, Met Office Hadley Centre and University of East Anglia, re-based to 1850–1900. Average for 2015–2024: 1.26 °C.
  3. Future warming: IPCC AR6 WG1 Summary for Policymakers, Table SPM.1 and Figure SPM.6.
  4. Product design: SEWA, Climate Resilience for All, ICICI Lombard and Swiss Re heat cover, reported by NPR in July 2025.
  5. Cost of capital: Solvency II Delegated Regulation (EU) 2015/35, Article 39.
